About this map

How to read it, where the numbers come from, and what they mean — in plain terms.

This map puts two things side by side for each state: how many families use its school-choice program, and where private schools actually are. Together they answer one question — when families get school-choice funding, is there a private school near them to use it?

The two things being measured

  • Participation (the demand). How many students, accounts, or scholarships a program has — the colored areas on the map. Straight from each state's official program report.
  • Private schools (the supply). Where private schools are and how many students they hold — the dots on the map.

Where the numbers come from

  • Private-school lists: 17 states publish their own official private-school directory — we use those. For every other state we use the U.S. Department of Education's national private-school survey (NCES).
  • School size (seats): a few states report enrollment directly; for the rest we borrow it from the NCES survey (2023–24). About 1 in 7 schools is too new to appear in that survey and shows no seat count.
  • Program participation: each state's own school-choice program report.
  • Number of children: the U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey (ACS), used to turn raw counts into per-child rates.

What the headline stats mean

  • Take-up — the share of a state's school-age children who use the program. Higher means the program reaches more families.
  • Participants per private-school seat — program users divided by private-school seats. Below 1 means there are more seats than users (room to spare). Well above 1 means far more users than local private seats — a sign the money is going to homeschooling, microschools, or online, not brick-and-mortar private schools.
  • "No private school nearby" — the share of children who live in an area (zip, county, or district) that has no private school at all. This is the clearest measure of whether access is spread evenly or concentrated in a few places.

Things to keep in mind

  • Census and NCES figures are estimates and a couple of years old — good for comparing places, not exact head-counts.
  • Programs count different things (students vs. accounts vs. scholarships), so state totals aren't apples-to-apples.
  • A few areas are missing data, which can nudge access to look slightly better or worse than it is; we flag these where they matter.
  • Alaska and Hawaii sit in their own boxes (bottom-left) — click either to view it full-screen, and click the U.S. box to return.

Full technical source citations for every number are in the project's data/profiles/sources.json and the scripts/pipeline/ documentation.
